Ever asked one AI a question, then asked a second AI to check the answer?

When both agree, you stop worrying and you move on.

Here's the real reason that feels safe: we assume two different AIs fail on two different things.

So if one slips up, the other one should catch it.

That assumption is what one founder decided to test.

Josef Chen runs a food robotics startup in London, and working alone, he pushed **67** models from **21** providers through the same questions to measure how often they fail together.

And here's where it gets uncomfortable.

On the hardest science questions, **12.7%** of them defeated every single one of the **67** models together.

The standard maths the industry uses predicted less than half as much.

Here's why they fail together.

Every one of these models learned from the same internet, so they inherited the same blind spots.

A question sitting inside that shared blind spot doesn't just trip one model, it defeats all of them together.

And it gets worse the moment you let models vote.

Chen found that stacking weaker models around a strong one dropped the score by about **10** points, because the weak ones simply outvote the smart one.

And here's why that matters.

This is a ceiling written in maths, not a bug someone can patch.

No amount of clever routing gets you past the questions where everyone is wrong together.

So the next time two AIs agree with each other, remember what that agreement actually proves.

It proves they read the same internet.
