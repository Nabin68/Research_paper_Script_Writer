## Caption

In 1950, long before computers could do much more than basic math, one man asked a question that still defines AI today.
Can machines think?
His name was Alan Turing, and the paper was called Computing Machinery and Intelligence.
He could not answer it directly, so he designed a test instead. Put a human in one room and a machine in another. Ask both the same questions about creativity, logic, and strategy. If you cannot tell which answers came from the machine, then for all practical purposes, it is thinking.
But Turing added something most people forget. The machine could not start out smart. It had to learn first. He imagined what he called a child machine. Instead of programming every fact into it, you teach it like a kid. Reward the right answers, correct the wrong ones, and over time it starts learning on its own.
Today we call that reinforcement learning, and it is at the heart of how modern AI is trained.
The test itself was finally run in 1991, at Boston's Computer Museum. Ten judges, real humans and early chatbots. The result? Five of them were convinced a chatbot was a person.
Turing predicted all of this decades before the machines could prove him right.
