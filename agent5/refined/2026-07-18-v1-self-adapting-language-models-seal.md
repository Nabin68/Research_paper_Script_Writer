**[BODY]**

Ever feed an AI a new fact, and watch it forget the moment you close the chat?

Here's why: once a large language model finishes training, it's frozen — it can only "remember" something new through whatever's sitting in the prompt, never through real experience.

**MIT CSAIL** just built a system that fixes that, called **SEAL**, Self-Adapting Language Models.

Instead of waiting on a human to retrain it, the model writes its own "self-edit" — its own study notes, practice questions, even instructions for how it should be fine-tuned — then updates its own weights with that self-made material.

No human touches the weights.

And here's where it gets interesting.

On a simplified set of reasoning puzzles, that same setup scored **zero percent** with no adaptation at all.

Self-edits with no feedback loop pushed that to **20%**.

Add a loop that rewards the model whenever its self-edits actually raise its score, and it hit **72.5%**.

Zero, to twenty, to seventy-two point five — no human touched a single weight.

The model tries several different self-edits, keeps whichever style actually raised its score afterward, and gets better at teaching itself, not just at the original task.

That same loop showed up on a knowledge test, too.

With no adaptation at all, the model answered questions from memory at **32.7%** accuracy.

After **SEAL** let it self-edit and retrain twice, that climbed to **47.0%** — beating models fine-tuned directly on the raw passage, and even edging out models trained on notes written by **GPT-4.1**.

This flips the direction of AI progress.

Instead of only scaling up training data and compute, models start compounding on their own experience, permanently, with no human rewriting anything.
